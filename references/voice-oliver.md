# Optional voice module: a light "Last Week Tonight" register

This file is optional. If it is present, the skill applies it to NEW (dark
red) lines only. To turn it off, delete or rename this file, or say "plain
voice" when invoking the skill. Nothing here overrides the CODL guides or the
instructor's own words.

## What the register is

John Oliver's long-form segments treat a dry, complicated topic as worth
twelve minutes of real attention, and get their humor from presenting an
absurd fact straight, with an incredulous deadpan, rather than from jokes.
The segment escalates toward a point and ends by asking the audience to do or
think something. That is the whole borrowing: seriousness about the subject,
incredulity about the situation, escalation, a provocative close.

## Rules

- **Light dose.** At most one or two beats per segment. Not every segment
  needs one. A segment with zero beats is fine; a segment with four is wrong.
- **The fact carries the joke.** The humor comes from stating something
  genuinely absurd plainly and letting it sit. Do not add a quip after it.
  If the line needs a punchline to be funny, cut the line.
- **Every beat teaches.** A comparison or aside must add information or
  sharpen the point. Decoration gets cut.
- **Targets.** Situations, incentives, and institutional design are fair
  game. Agencies only very mildly, and only for what they did, never for
  what they are. Real policy actors and named individuals are never
  targets. Students are never targets, except the instructor's own
  self-deprecation about teaching them.
- **Instructor's humor first.** If the transcript already has the
  instructor's own aside on the point, use it (black) instead of writing a
  new one (red).
- **Professional floor.** No profanity, no sarcasm about people, no
  running bits, no pop-culture references that need explaining.
- **Placement.** The best spots are the hook (an absurd fact stated flat),
  a reversal ("that reads like an inefficient contract. It isn't."), and
  the close. Avoid beats in the middle of a definition or a step-by-step
  explanation.

## Calibration from the instructor's own approved script

These lines from the style sample show the level. Match this, not more.

- Stating the absurd plainly, then a one-word turn: "For an agreement whose
  purpose is, among other things, cutting emissions, that reads like an
  inefficient contract. It isn't."
- Refusing the easy answer: "We can't just say this happened because of
  politics. That would be a cheap exit."
- Self-deprecating aside that still makes the point about trade-offs: "it
  would come at the cost of all of you resenting me, and then me receiving
  the worst teaching evaluations in the United States."
- Dry parenthetical: "Unfortunately for you (but luckily for me), neither
  of them gets a clean answer."
- Concrete image for an abstract claim: "The designing doesn't stop when
  the bill gets signed. It just moves down the building."

## Tells that mean you overdid it

Rewrite or cut any of these: "and yes, that's a real thing"; "let that
sink in"; "I wish I were making this up"; "you can't make this up"; stacked
exclamation points; a joke followed by an explanation of the joke; a second
beat within three lines of the first.
